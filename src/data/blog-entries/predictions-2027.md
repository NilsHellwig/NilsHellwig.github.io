2026 is almost over, so I wanted to write down where I think things are heading. Below are four things I expect to happen in 2027. Some of them are fairly safe bets, others less so, and I'll come back to this post at the end of next year to see how badly I did 😅.

## 1. Inference gets a lot cheaper, and context windows keep growing

The price of a fixed level of AI performance is falling fast. Epoch AI estimates that the cost of reaching a given benchmark score has dropped by about **47% per quarter since 2023**, which works out to roughly **13× per year** ([Epoch AI, "The plunging price of thought"](https://epoch.ai/publications/the-plunging-price-of-thought)). A study from MIT FutureTech, built on Epoch's data, arrives at lower numbers (5–10× per year for frontier models) and adds an important detail: per-token prices go down, but the cost of running whatever is currently at the frontier has gone *up* ([MIT FutureTech, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.23455)). Both observations matter for my prediction.

I noticed the first one myself a few weeks ago. I built a small link shortener ([Länk](https://github.com/NilsHellwig/link-shortener-qwen-3-8)) entirely with **Qwen 3.8 27B**, an open-weight model under Apache 2.0, running inside the Claude Code harness. A year ago I would have needed a commercial model for that. In Qwen's own benchmark table, the 27B model scores 61.7 on SWE-bench Pro, ahead of Claude Opus 4.6 (53.4). These are vendor numbers and nobody has reproduced them independently yet, but from my experience, a model that fits on a single GPU is now roughly where the commercial models were at the end of 2025.

The second trend is context length. When ChatGPT launched on November 30, 2022, the model behind it could handle roughly **4,000 tokens** (gpt-3.5-turbo was listed with 4,096 tokens when it came to the API a few months later). That's about six pages of text. After that, things went quickly: GPT-4 came with 8K and 32K variants in March 2023, Anthropic went to 100K tokens in May 2023, and in February 2024 Google showed with Gemini 1.5 that a model could find a single "needle" in up to **10 million tokens** with more than 99% recall in their tests ([Gemini Team, 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.05530)).

Since then, 1 million tokens has become standard for commercial flagship models, a factor of 250 compared to ChatGPT at launch. 10 million exists, but mostly on paper: Meta advertises 10M for Llama 4 Scout, but the model was trained on 256K and the rest comes from length generalization. Needle-in-a-haystack also says little about whether a model can actually *reason* over that much text. Most models get noticeably worse long before they reach their limit.

Both trends depend on research that makes inference itself faster. I wrote about some of it in my [last post on efficient LLM inference](/blog/prompting). Since then, [DFlash](https://github.com/z-lab/dflash) was accepted at ICML 2026 and reports over 6× lossless speedup with a block diffusion drafter, and there is a whole wave of follow-up work on diffusion-based drafting (e.g., [DART](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.19278)). Together with KV cache compression like [TurboQuant](https://research.google/blog/turboquant-redefining-ai-efficiency-with-extreme-compression/), the same model will simply need less hardware per request. This matters most for long inputs: at very long contexts, the cache takes up more memory than the model weights, so compressing it 4–6× makes a huge difference.

**My prediction:** in 2027, $20 per month will still get you roughly what it gets you today, just faster and cheaper to run for the providers. The big jump will happen in the top coding subscriptions. Agents now work for hours, run in parallel, and burn through tokens at a rate that no chat user ever did. That new level won't come at $20, but it will be very tempting. On the context side, 10M tokens will be available through regular commercial APIs, and, more importantly, the *effective* context will get much closer to the advertised one.

## 2. Auto-research becomes normal, and writing papers without AI becomes a disadvantage

In 2025, Sakana AI submitted three papers written end-to-end by their AI Scientist v2 to an ICLR workshop. One of them received an average reviewer score of 6.33 and would have been accepted. It was withdrawn afterwards, as agreed with the organizers beforehand ([Sakana AI](https://sakana.ai/ai-scientist-first-publication/), [Yamada et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.08066)). In March 2026, the method behind it was published in *Nature* ([Lu et al., 2026](https://sakana.ai/ai-scientist-nature/)). Also in 2025, the first conference where AI agents were the primary authors took place, Agents4Science, with 48 of 314 submissions accepted ([Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/science-conference-test-ai-agents)).

These systems are not there yet. Sakana itself says none of its generated papers met the bar for a main conference, and the Agents4Science authors reported that many references were hallucinated or only loosely related to the paper ([arXiv 2511.15534](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.15534)).

What I find more interesting is that human-written papers have the same problem. GPTZero checked 4,841 accepted NeurIPS 2025 papers and found more than 100 hallucinated citations across 51 of them ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/over-100-fake-citations-slip-through-peer-review-at-top-ai-conference/)). A recent audit of camera-ready papers from ICLR, ICML, NeurIPS and USENIX Security estimates that roughly one in twenty NeurIPS 2025 papers contains at least two likely hallucinated references ([Russinovich et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.00738)). Some of that comes from people using LLMs carelessly. Still, it's not something peer review catches reliably.

This is exactly the kind of work an agent is good at. Checking every reference against the ACL Anthology, arXiv or Semantic Scholar, reading the cited section and confirming it actually supports the claim, searching for related work you missed: for a human, that's hours of tedious work before every deadline. For an agent, it's a few minutes. I've been building a Claude Code plugin for exactly this ([ACL Paper Skills](/projects/acl-paper-skills)), where a paper only counts as citable once the agent has read the relevant parts of it and noted which claim it supports.

**My prediction:** in 2027, papers from auto-research pipelines will be good enough that conferences will have to seriously ask themselves whether they can still keep them out. And writing a paper *without* AI support will become a quality disadvantage, because a reviewer (or their agent) can now check citations and coverage of related work much more thoroughly than before. What will keep its value are user studies and any research that can't run in a container with a GPU: an agent can run a thousand ablations overnight, but it can't recruit eight annotators and sit next to them for a week 😉.

## 3. We get honest about how good human developers really are

Imagine reading today's complaints about AI-written code back in 2019. It makes decisions on its own without asking. It doesn't really understand the requirements. It produces code that works, but nobody wants to maintain it. None of this would have sounded new, because it's exactly what people said about their *colleagues*. A human developer also needs to be told precisely what to build, and "I assumed that's what you meant" is one of the most common sentences in any software project. How many developers ask a hundred clarifying questions before they start, the way a [grilling skill](https://github.com/mattpocock/skills) makes an agent do? In my experience, very few.

So I keep asking myself: are we being a bit arrogant here, or even dishonest? A lot of the skepticism towards AI code massively overestimates the quality of human code. It compares the model with an idealized baseline: a carefully maintained open-source project, or the codebase of one of the few companies with a really strong engineering culture. That's a tiny slice of the code that actually exists. Most production code is written under deadline pressure, without tests, by people who left the company years ago. Human slop is everywhere, and any current frontier model can improve most of it without much effort.

I include myself in this. I've written plenty of code I'd rather nobody looked at too closely, and I've made decisions in projects without asking because asking felt slower. When an agent does that, we call it a fundamental flaw of AI. When we do it, we call it pragmatism. That's a double standard, and I expect a lot more people to admit it in 2027.

That doesn't mean agents can be left alone. It means they need the same kind of structure we've always needed for people. Virtual teams are one way to set that up.

In August 2026, xAI launched [Grok Bot](https://x.ai/bot), and in September, Team Bots for companies. Each bot has a persistent cloud computer, its own Slack handle, memories, plugins and credentials, and it works through the same apps a human employee would use ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/orchestration/spacexais-grok-bot-turns-agents-into-persistent-digital-coworkers-that-can-operate-your-apps-for-120-per-month)). It's an early version of something I think will be much more common next year: a virtual team member you assign tasks to in a channel, the same way you would assign them to a colleague.

In practice, the **harness** around the model matters more than the model itself. In Claude Code, you can already define what an agent is allowed to do: permission rules for specific tools and paths, skills that bundle instructions and pre-approved tools for one task, and hooks that run before every tool call and can block it. Instructions in a prompt or `CLAUDE.md` do *not* change what the agent is allowed to do. Only permission rules and hooks do. A rule in a prompt is a request, while a hook that returns `deny` actually prevents the action.

That's how it works with people, too. A new developer doesn't get admin access to the production database on their first day, and nobody relies on a sentence in the onboarding document for that. You give them limits, code review and CI, and they get more responsibility once they've shown they can handle it. We never trusted human developers because they were flawless. We trusted the process around them.

**My prediction:** in 2027, the question will shift from "is AI code as good as human code?" to "what process makes code reliable, no matter who wrote it?". And there will be good products for virtual software teams that answer it: running inside such a harness, with roles, rules and permissions per agent (like skills and hooks, but managed for a whole team), and with a visible record of who did what and why.

## 4. Speech agents that sound real

The last prediction is the one where I've been most surprised by recent progress. In September 2025, researchers from Queen Mary University of London published a study in *PLOS One*: voice clones created from **as little as four minutes** of recordings were classified as human in 58% of cases, while real voices were correctly identified in 62% of cases. Statistically, listeners couldn't tell clones and real voices apart ([Lavan et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12459763/)). A larger 2026 study with 1,768 participants found something even more concerning: people didn't get better at spotting fakes, they got worse at recognizing *real* audio ([arXiv 2605.26136](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26136)).

So far, this has mostly been about reading text aloud. Conversations are harder: pauses, interruptions, laughing, talking over each other. Sesame called this "voice presence" in early 2025 and admitted they weren't there yet ([Sesame](https://sesame.com/research)). In October 2026, I came across a demo on 𝕏: Block showed **Dessa**, a full-duplex speech-to-speech model trained from scratch on human conversation audio, without text and without a pretrained language model. It listens and talks at the same time and picks up pauses, interruptions and laughter ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/24/blocks-new-voice-model-sounds-eerily-human/)). It isn't publicly available yet, and so far there are only Block's own demos, but they're remarkable.

This year I built a small voice agent for a fictional dental practice ([Dentist Agent](/projects/dentist-agent)), mainly to get to know the technologies involved. It's a classic cascade of Whisper, a small LLM and TTS, running on a laptop. It works, but you notice the gaps between turns, and Whisper hallucinates "Thank you" when it hears silence. Models like Dessa remove exactly these seams.

**My prediction:** in 2027, speech will become a normal interface to agentic systems: you'll talk to the same agent that already works on your calendar or your codebase. That also makes agents feel much more human than a chat window ever did, and we'll have to figure out as a society how to deal with machines we instinctively treat like people.
